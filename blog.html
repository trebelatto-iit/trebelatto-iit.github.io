On the Virtue of Beginning Badly

February 24, 2026

There is a peculiar tyranny in the blank page — not because it lacks possibility, but because it holds too much. Every sentence you might write exists there, suspended in potential, and the act of choosing one destroys all the others. Writers have long understood this. What they discuss less often is the remedy: beginning badly, on purpose.

A bad first sentence is an act of mercy toward yourself. It does not ask to be great. It asks only to exist — to break the silence, to put something where before there was nothing. The page stops being infinite the moment you mark it. And from something finite, however rough, you can work.

"The first draft of anything is shit." — Ernest Hemingway

This principle extends well beyond writing. The first sketch is not the drawing. The first prototype is not the product. The first draft of a conversation, rehearsed in the shower, is not the conversation you will actually have. We know this, abstractly. We resist it, practically, because we confuse the quality of the beginning with the quality of what the beginning might become.

There is something almost philosophical about the resistance. To begin is to commit — not to the outcome, but to the process. And process is unglamorous. It involves wrong turns, abandoned lines, paragraphs that looked promising at midnight and embarrassing by morning. The person who begins badly accepts all of this in advance. That acceptance is, itself, a kind of courage.

Earth, seen from a distance. Everything began here.

Give yourself permission to make something disposable. The good work tends to follow — not because bad beginnings magically transform, but because the habit of starting, of making peace with imperfection, is the only reliable path to anything finished. The page that exists, however rough, is infinitely more workable than the page that doesn't.

Begin badly. Begin today.
